Gatefy Making complex email security feel controllable

Led end-to-end UX/UI for a high-tech B2B cybersecurity platform, transforming complex security rules into an intuitive, user-friendly experience. Defined the MVP and built a scalable Design System to ensure secure and efficient administration workflows.

The Background

Gatefy started as a reseller for major security gateways like Proofpoint, Sophos, and McAfee. By analyzing these tools, the team realized a huge market opportunity: creating a new product that combined the strengths of the competitors. Initially allocated to the Marketing team, I identified that all these legacy tools were outdated, clunky, and not intuitive. I pitched a strategic shift to transition into the Product team, championing a user-centered approach and proving that a seamless UX would be our biggest differentiator and competitive advantage in the market.

Problem

Gatefy needed to present a large set of security capabilities, policies, and configuration options without overwhelming IT and security admins. In early explorations, navigation depth and inconsistent patterns made it hard to quickly locate settings, verify system status, and troubleshoot issues. This created risk: admins hesitated to change rules they did not fully understand, and it increased time to complete critical tasks. Competitors offered simpler surfaces, so Gatefy had to prove clarity without sacrificing power. Beyond the interface design, my role involved championing UX as a strategic business differentiator, transforming a reseller mindset into a product-driven company.

Solution

I designed an information architecture and navigation model that kept advanced features accessible while reducing cognitive load. I defined an MVP around the most common admin workflows, then built reusable UI patterns and a design system to keep behavior consistent across menus and submenus. Prototypes focused on fast scanning, clear system status, and safe configuration through progressive disclosure, sensible defaults, and predictable layout. The result positioned Gatefy as a platform that feels controlled and understandable, even when the feature set stays deep.

Methodology

I applied a User-Centered Design (UCD) approach to balance three critical pillars: user needs, business goals, and technology viability. In a high-tech cybersecurity platform, it's easy to over-engineer for technology and forget the humans operating it. By working closely with engineering and stakeholders, we co-created solutions that made complex security rules accessible without compromising the system's robustness. The goal was to generate an innovative, intuitive experience that empowered IT admins to work confidently.

Double Diamond Stages

I applied the Double Diamond framework to ensure a user-centered approach without losing focus on technological feasibility and business goals. In the first diamond, we focused on Discovery and Analysis—conducting benchmarking and stakeholder interviews to define the real problem. In the second diamond, we moved into Ideation, Prototyping, and Usability Testing, ensuring the complex security features were validated with users before development. Benchmarking

To understand the market landscape, I conducted a UX benchmarking analysis of the main competitors (Sophos, McAfee, and Proofpoint). The goal was to identify their strengths and, more importantly, their UX weaknesses. The analysis confirmed that while these tools were powerful, they suffered from outdated visual design, fragmented navigation, and high cognitive load.

To structure our strategy, I also facilitated a SWOT analysis with the team. We identified that competitors had strong security features (Strengths) but terrible usability (Weaknesses). This exposed a massive market opportunity (Opportunities) to create a product that matched their technical power but was radically easier to use. The main threat (Threats) was the high switching cost for enterprise clients, which we planned to mitigate through a seamless, low-friction migration experience.

This gave us a clear direction: Gatefy needed to match their security capabilities but deliver a radically simpler and more intuitive user experience.

Interviews

To build a product that could outperform legacy competitors, I conducted interviews with a diverse group of stakeholders. This included a beta testing client (and their end-users) to understand daily operations, my manager (who had deep sales experience with competitor products), and the support team. By combining user feedback with insights from sales and support, we mapped the exact pain points of legacy tools (like poor navigation and lack of feedback) to ensure our MVP would deliver a superior, intuitive experience.

FINDING 1: Competitor Gap

Legacy competitors (Proofpoint, Sophos) were powerful but notoriously complex and unintuitive. Users were desperate for a tool that didn't require a steep learning curve.

FINDING 2: Cognitive Overload

Beta users struggled with cognitive overload. Creating security rules was scary because they didn't understand the system's feedback, leading to fear of breaking the email flow.

FINDING 3: Support Burden

The support and sales teams confirmed that the lack of clear visual status and fragmented workflows were the main reasons clients abandoned legacy tools or opened repetitive tickets.

Personas

I created two personas based on customer profile rather than on a specific product or feature, so they remain relevant as the product evolves. They were built collaboratively with the design team and other areas of the company.

Together, they represent two roles within the same organization: an IT analyst who operates security tools day to day, and an information security director responsible for broader decisions. Despite their different responsibilities, both share the same core pains: too much time spent on tasks that should be simple, little visibility into what happens inside the email gateway, and limited organizational support to modernize their area. These insights guided MVP scope decisions, navigation labels, and the level of clarity needed at critical moments of the workflow.

Customer Journey

I mapped the end-to-end customer journey to understand the full lifecycle: from the initial frustration with legacy tools to the product evaluation, purchase decision, and migration. This map revealed critical pain points—such as the complexity of comparing features and the fear of migrating email flows. These insights helped shape the MVP, ensuring the onboarding and configuration screens were as seamless as the daily monitoring.

Sketches

Before writing any code or jumping into high-fidelity UI, I used quick hand-drawn sketches to explore the information architecture and the new navigation model. This low-fidelity approach allowed me to rapidly iterate on the placement of core features (like search, profile, and configurations) and validate the mental model with the team. It saved significant time and avoided painful adjustments later in the design process.

User Flow

To manage the massive volume of security features and submenus without overwhelming the user, I designed a robust Information Architecture. The User Flow was structured to keep daily operational tasks (like Search, Messages, and Dashboard) accessible at any time from the sidebar, while complex configurations were grouped logically into modular categories (Administration, System, Protection, Data Security). This structure minimized navigation depth and made it easier for IT admins to find the exact setting they needed without getting lost.

UI Design

The final UI Design focused on clarity and reducing cognitive load. I designed a clean, dark-themed interface that contrasted heavily with the outdated, clunky legacy competitors. By applying the Design System components, complex workflows—such as managing user settings and configuring additional users—were translated into a logical, step-by-step modal layout. This ensured that IT admins could configure advanced security rules with confidence, without feeling overwhelmed.

Design System

I built a comprehensive Design System to serve as the foundation for the product's build, rather than just a one-off prototype. The process was highly collaborative and iterative; I maintained continuous alignment with the Product Owner and Engineering team to ensure new features, inputs, and components were added logically.

By establishing clear rules for colors, typography, interactive states (hover, disabled, error), and drag-and-drop components, we drastically reduced design debt. This scalable system made it significantly easier for both designers and developers to maintain consistency across the complex security modules.

MMP & MVP

During my time on the project, the product went from concept to its first release, passing through the definition of the MVP and MMP.

To ensure a fast market launch without compromising quality, we adopted an MMP (Minimum Marketable Product) strategy. My role involved collaborating closely with stakeholders to evaluate which features aligned with our strategic goals and technical viability, moving complex edge cases to the post-release backlog. We ranked features by how essential they were: protecting the customer’s mailbox is the foundation of any email gateway, so that came first, and the product could not launch without it.

I prioritized the core workflows that guaranteed a high-quality user experience: user management, basic email flow configuration, and dashboard monitoring. This MVP allowed IT admins to operate the system safely from day one, while we gathered real feedback to iterate on advanced features.

A critical realization during this phase was that in cybersecurity, usability cannot exist without trust: if the product fails to protect the user’s data, the UX work means nothing. Therefore, the MVP focused on delivering a secure, reliable configuration experience before expanding to deeper features.

Some of the features deferred to the backlog were already planned while I was on the team and were implemented after I left. Today, this logic is still visible in the product: customers can stay with the essentials or contract some features as add-ons.

Learnings & Key Takeaways

  • Building a product from scratch was a massive learning experience. It reinforced that planning, research, and continuous experimentation are non-negotiable to design experiences that are truly simple, intuitive, and pleasant.
  • One of the most valuable lessons was embracing mistakes as part of the process. Iterating and failing fast is crucial for improvement, especially when grounded in solid academic methods and user-centered frameworks.
  • Finally, it is essential to fall in love with the user's problem, not your first solution. Being open to deconstructing ideas and pivoting when necessary is what allows a team to reach the best possible outcome for the product and the user.
